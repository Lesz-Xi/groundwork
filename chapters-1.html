Research is a disciplined way of changing your mind.

In plain words. You begin with something you do not adequately understand. You investigate it in a way that makes the answer more than a persuasive guess—and leaves a trail another person can inspect.

Research is not synonymous with searching, reading, collecting citations, writing a paper, or producing something novel. Those can be parts of research. The load-bearing operation is the connection between a question, a method, evidence or argument, and a conclusion whose limits remain visible. The exact meaning of research varies across disciplines: an archive, an interview, an experiment, a mathematical proof and a designed artifact can each carry inquiry, but not the same kind of warrant.

A useful working loop is question → investigation → interpretation → criticism → revision. This is a teaching model, not a claim that every project follows five tidy stages. You may discover that your question is ill-posed, your measure misses the phenomenon, or an older result already answers it. Revising the question can be progress, not failure. Philosophical accounts of scientific method examine this diversity rather than giving every field one mechanical recipe. [S01]

Learning, investigation and contribution

You can do careful research to learn what is already known. An original research contribution additionally changes what others can know, explain, measure or do. A literature synthesis can contribute by resolving a disagreement or exposing a boundary; a replication can strengthen or challenge confidence without inventing a new mechanism. Personal learning and public novelty are therefore distinct achievements.

Do not begin by promising certainty. Begin by identifying what kind of uncertainty you can responsibly reduce. A defensible negative result, a counterexample, a narrower claim, an improved measurement or a well-supported account of why evidence is insufficient can all be useful outputs.

The four questions underneath the work

  1. What exactly am I trying to find out?
  2. What observations, sources or arguments would bear on it?
  3. Why does my method connect those materials to an answer?
  4. What would make the answer weaker, wrong or inapplicable?

Keep this distinction: information becomes evidence only in relation to a question and an inference. A folder full of documents is not yet an argument.

Try it

Write one thing you believe about your project, then finish: “I would reconsider this if…” If nothing could change it, identify whether you are stating a preference, a definition, a value commitment or a testable claim.

“Extensive” is not the same as “rigorous.”

In plain words. Visiting many places is breadth. Studying one place carefully is depth. Making your route and judgments defensible is rigor. You need the combination that fits your question.

ExtensiveA large reach or amount of work: many sources, cases, contexts or questions.Depth, correct inference or a complete search.
ComprehensiveCoverage of the important dimensions within a declared scope.That every possible source was found.
ExhaustiveAn ambition to identify all relevant material in a defined search universe.Literal omniscience about all languages, archives and unpublished work.

Exclusive means restricted to some people or things, or excluding others. It is not a synonym for exhaustive. “Not an exhaustive literature or prior-art review” is a coverage disclosure: selected sources were used, but the surrounding work has not been mapped comprehensively enough to make unrestricted priority claims.

Make the denominator visible

“We searched the literature” hides the search universe. A useful statement names the topic, eligible source types, databases or collections, languages, date range, last search date, and important access limitations. Breadth has a cost: more material to screen, reconcile and keep current. Narrowness has a cost too: excluded contexts can contain the closest precedent or strongest counterexample. [S04]

A sound stopping rule is not “I feel finished.” It might be completion of a prespecified search across selected sources plus citation chasing and assessment of known gaps. Iterative theoretical or qualitative work can use different stopping rationales, but must explain them. Budget exhaustion is a legitimate constraint when disclosed; it is not evidence of completeness.

Do not infer: 200 citations means a stronger paper than 20 carefully chosen, deeply understood sources. Counts measure collection size, not warrant.

Try it

Complete: “This investigation is comprehensive with respect to ___, but does not cover ___.” If you cannot name both, the scope is still vague.

Move from a topic to an answerable question.

In plain words. “AI and research” is a territory. “Under what conditions does this interface help a reviewer detect stale evidence?” gives you something to investigate.

A topic names an area. A problem names something not adequately understood or served. A question states what you want to learn. An objective states what you will accomplish. A hypothesis offers a candidate answer that can meet criticism. A contribution is what the completed work adds—not merely what you intend to build.

Choose the kind of answer

  • Descriptive: what exists, how often, or in what forms?
  • Comparative: how do groups, cases, methods or interpretations differ?
  • Predictive: how well can we anticipate an outcome on genuinely new cases?
  • Causal: what would change under a specified intervention?
  • Interpretive: what meanings or practices organize experience in context?
  • Formal: what follows from these definitions and assumptions?
  • Constructive/design: what artifact can meet these requirements, and how will that be evaluated?
  • Normative: what ought to be done, under which defensible values and reasons?

These categories can interact, but their evidence obligations differ. Prediction does not automatically identify causes. Interviews do not estimate population prevalence without an appropriate sampling/inference design. A prototype demonstrates that something can be built, not that it improves outcomes. Data can inform a normative judgment without supplying its values by themselves.

Give the question edges

Name the population or setting, object of study, comparison where relevant, outcome or concept, timescale, and unit of analysis. Frameworks such as PICO can help with some intervention questions; they are aids, not universal molds for mathematics, history or interpretive work. Ask whether access, time, expertise and ethical permissions make the question feasible.

Start with one main question and a small number of dependent questions. If each subquestion demands a different dataset, population and disciplinary method, you may have several projects rather than one ambitious paper.

The question governs the method. Do not pick a fashionable tool and then retrofit a question merely to justify using it.

Try it

Rewrite your topic as a question with an explicit object, context and answer type. Then name one answer your planned method could not justify.

Your method already contains a view of the world.

In plain words. Before measuring something, you have made choices about what it is, what counts as knowing it, and what matters. Make those choices inspectable.

Ontology concerns what kinds of things exist or are treated as real in an inquiry. Epistemology concerns what counts as knowledge and how it can be justified. Methodology is the rationale and logic connecting questions to methods. Methods are the actual procedures: interviewing, sampling, coding, proving, modeling or testing. Axiology concerns the role of values. These are not decorative opening-section words; they matter when they change what evidence you seek or which conclusion you can draw. [S01]

For example, treating “understanding” as a benchmark score is not merely choosing a convenient metric. It is adopting an operational representation of a contested concept. You may justify that representation for a narrow task without claiming it exhausts the phenomenon. Ask what it reveals, what it discards and what would invalidate the mapping.

Three useful reasoning movements

  • Deduction: deriving what must follow if stated premises hold. Soundness needs valid inference and appropriate premises.
  • Induction: generalizing beyond observed cases. Its warrant depends on how the cases and world support that extension.
  • Abduction: developing a plausible explanation for observations. The best current explanation remains open to rivals and new evidence.

Exploration generates possibilities. Confirmation tests specified expectations. Neither is inherently superior; hiding an exploratory choice as if it were a prior prediction is the problem. Preregistration can make that distinction more visible, but it does not make a weak theory or measurement strong. [S05]

Representation is a research decision

A transcript makes chronology easy to recover. A claim–source table makes support easy to inspect. A causal model makes intervention assumptions explicit. A proof-dependency graph makes lemmas visible. None is interchangeable with the others. In particular, a workflow arrow or dependency edge is not automatically a causal relationship.

Assumptions are load-bearing. The goal is not to have none; it is to know which ones your conclusion depends on and how vulnerable they are.

Try it

Name one construct in your project. Describe it in ordinary language, then write how you intend to observe or reason about it. What gets lost between those two descriptions?

A review type is a commitment, not a prestige label.

In plain words. You might need a guided introduction, a map of a field, or a carefully bounded answer to a narrow question. Those are different jobs.

PRISMA is a reporting guideline: it helps readers see why a review was done, what was done and what was found. A completed checklist or flow diagram does not certify excellent conduct, absence of bias or a true conclusion. PRISMA-ScR is the related extension for scoping reviews; the extension should not be confused with the main reporting guideline. Choose the relevant guidance for the actual review type. [S02] [S03]

Do not label a handful of web searches a systematic review because the final article has a PRISMA-style diagram. Conversely, a carefully scoped narrative synthesis can be valuable without that label. Review work has its own research question and method; it is not the preliminary reading stage stretched into a paper.

Conduct guidance and reporting guidance are complementary. The Cochrane Handbook provides detailed methods for intervention reviews. Its procedures are useful evidence for that domain, not a command to force every historical or theoretical inquiry into a clinical-review template. [S04]

Choose honestly: the right name describes the work actually performed. It does not upgrade the work.

Try it

Is your immediate aim to understand an idea, map available evidence, estimate an effect, or establish a specific prior-work difference? Pick the corresponding review operation before choosing a label.

Look backward before claiming a new contribution.

In plain words. Prior-art review asks what others have already done. Novelty assessment asks what meaningful difference remains in your own work after that comparison.

In academic and architectural work, relevant prior work may be a paper, technical report, specification, documented system, implementation or earlier conceptual argument. The newest paper is not necessarily the closest precedent. You are looking for work that shares the problem, mechanism, assumptions, operating conditions or contribution, including work described using different vocabulary.

“Prior art” also has specific legal meanings in patent practice. This handbook uses it in the broader research-comparison sense; it does not perform a patent search, determine patentability or offer legal advice. Academic originality and a legal novelty determination are not interchangeable.

Four questions that sharpen the comparison

  1. What is the closest existing approach—not merely a convenient weak baseline?
  2. Under which assumptions and conditions does it work?
  3. What precisely changes in our approach: mechanism, guarantee, integration, representation, evidence or application?
  4. What supports that difference, and why does it matter?

A gap is not yet a contribution. “Nobody studied this exact combination” may reflect lack of importance, an obvious extension, inaccessible data or different terminology. Explain why filling the gap improves understanding or practice. A contribution can be a carefully evaluated combination, a boundary result, a negative result, replication or improved measurement; it need not claim every component is unprecedented.

A safer novelty sentence

Instead of “This is the first system to make research trustworthy,” write a bounded comparison: “Unlike the selected approaches A and B, this proposal explicitly separates artifact-version review from action authorization; whether that separation improves review outcomes remains to be evaluated.” This is a synthetic phrasing example, not a finding about any named system.

Finding no predecessor in a limited search supports “we did not identify one within this search,” not “none exists.” Record searched terms, sources, dates, exclusions and unresolved neighbors before making priority claims.

Novel is not automatically useful; useful is not automatically novel. Give each claim its own argument and evidence.

Try it

Create a table with columns: closest work, shared problem, mechanism, assumptions, our difference, evidence for the difference, remaining uncertainty. Leave cells unknown rather than filling them with confident adjectives.

Search concepts, not just your favorite phrasing.

In plain words. Different communities can study the same problem under different names. A good search gives those neighboring vocabularies a chance to answer you.

Break the question into a few concept blocks. For each, list synonyms, older terms, acronyms, spelling variants and broader/narrower concepts. Combine alternatives within a block using OR; combine different concepts using AND where the database supports it. Phrase searching, truncation, proximity syntax, fields and controlled vocabulary differ across systems. Test syntax rather than transplanting a query blindly. [S04]

("human review" OR "human oversight")
AND ("version history" OR provenance OR "artifact version")
AND ("research assistant" OR "AI agent")

This is an illustrative query, not a search executed for an exhaustive architecture review. An AND-heavy query can miss papers that discuss only part of your mechanism. Run focused subqueries as well as combination queries. Use known relevant seed documents to check whether the strategy can retrieve material it ought to find.

Broaden the source channels deliberately

Use appropriate scholarly indexes and disciplinary databases, library catalogues, proceedings, preprint servers, registries, standards and relevant technical repositories. General web search is useful for discovery, but rankings are not a sampling frame. Backward citation chasing follows a paper's references; forward chasing finds later work that cites it. Both can reveal vocabulary and debates, but can also overrepresent well-connected schools.

Grey literature includes material outside conventional commercial academic publishing, such as reports, theses and some working papers. It can reduce publication blind spots while creating different appraisal and discovery challenges. Preprints provide early access but should be labeled with version and review status.

Keep a search log

Record the exact query, platform, fields/filters, date, coverage limits, returned/exported counts when actually observed, and what changed between searches. A DOI or metadata match identifies a record; it does not mean you read the article, evaluated its method or reproduced its result.

Search is iterative, but not unaccountable. Preserve the route so another person can understand both what you found and what you were unlikely to find.

Try it

Find three neighboring terms for your central idea. Predict which discipline would use each, then compare the kinds of sources each query retrieves.

Decide what belongs before the answer becomes convenient.

In plain words. Selection rules protect you from keeping only the sources that make your preferred conclusion look good.

Eligibility criteria say which questions, populations, contexts, methods, outcomes, dates or source types qualify. Inclusion and exclusion rules should serve the question, not the direction of the results. Pilot them on a small set to find ambiguity. If they change during the review, record why, when and which earlier decisions need revisiting. Systematic-review practice makes the search and selection trail explicit. [S02] [S04]

Distinguish a record found in a database, a report or publication, and the underlying study. One study may produce multiple reports; the same report may appear in several databases. Deduplicating citations does not automatically prevent double-counting participants or evidence. Corrections, translations, preprints and final publications also need version-aware handling.

Screen in stages, preserve reasons

Title/abstract screening is triage. Full-text assessment decides eligibility when the abstract is insufficient. “Full text unavailable” differs from “ineligible after reading.” Track retrieval failures rather than silently assigning exclusion reasons. Use relevant reporting standards to show what entered, left and remained in the process; do not invent flow counts after the fact.

Independent screening and extraction can reduce particular reviewer errors, but require a clear disagreement-resolution process and an honest description of independence. Two agents sharing the same prompt, context and model may repeat the same mistake. Neither multiple reviewers nor a software-assisted workflow guarantees unbiased selection.

Two threats worth naming

Selection bias can arise when the included evidence systematically differs from the relevant universe. Publication bias arises when availability is related to results or their perceived interest. Language, database and access restrictions may introduce additional asymmetries. A useful limitations section explains how those mechanisms might change your conclusion—not simply that “more research is needed.”

Absence has a history. A missing source, an excluded study and evidence of no effect are three different things.

Try it

Take one excluded source. Could a second reviewer reconstruct the reason from your record without asking what you remember?

Extract the argument, not just memorable sentences.

In plain words. Ask what the source claims, what supports it, what assumptions connect the two, and where the claim stops working.

Read at different resolutions. First orient: question, contribution, study type or argument structure, and relevance. Then examine methods, definitions and evidence for the claims you intend to use. Finally reconstruct the critical steps and compare them with alternatives. You need not read every reference cover to cover, but must not imply whole-source reading after inspecting only a title, abstract or excerpt.

Keep four voices separate

  • Quotation: the source's exact words, with an exact location.
  • Paraphrase: your faithful restatement, still attributed.
  • Interpretation: what you think the source means or implies.
  • Synthesis: a new connection or conclusion you draw across sources.

Paraphrasing is not deleting the citation. A sentence can be original in wording and still borrow its idea or evidence. Check qualifications: “may,” “under these assumptions,” “in this sample,” “pointwise,” “on average” and “within the model” are often the load-bearing part of a result.

A useful extraction record

Save identity/version, inspected pages or sections, the consequential claim, design/data or proof structure, assumptions, uncertainty, limitations, competing interpretations and the precise role the source plays in your work. For quantitative studies, extract the outcome definition, estimate, uncertainty, population, comparison and relevant design details together. For qualitative work, preserve context, participant perspective and the path from material to interpretation. For mathematics, preserve quantifiers and dependencies.

A claim ledger is not a truth machine. A hash identifies bytes; a citation resolves a document; a reviewer may still misunderstand either. The ledger helps locate the next correction. When a claim changes, revisit the dependent prose and checks instead of merely editing a bibliography entry.

Fluency can hide a missing step. If you cannot explain how the evidence supports the claim, you have a reading obligation—not a prose-polishing problem.

Try it

For one paper, write a five-sentence reconstruction: question, claim, support, assumptions, limitation. Mark which sentences are source reports and which are your interpretation.

Different questions need different kinds of contact with reality.

In plain words. A thermometer, an interview and a proof answer different questions. Sophistication is choosing the appropriate instrument, not always choosing the most technical-looking one.

Quantitative approaches represent aspects of a phenomenon numerically and use mathematical/statistical reasoning. Qualitative approaches investigate meaning, experience, practice, process and context through appropriate interpretive procedures. Mixed methods intentionally integrates quantitative and qualitative components so their relationship answers a question neither handles adequately alone. Adding one quotation to a survey does not, by itself, establish a mixed-methods design.

Experiments manipulate conditions; observational studies do not assign the exposure of interest in the same way. Surveys can measure reported attitudes or behavior but inherit question, sampling and response problems. Case studies examine bounded cases in depth; ethnographic approaches examine practices in social contexts. Historical work critically interprets records, provenance, absence and context. Formal work uses definitions and argument; design research constructs and evaluates artifacts. These are families, not mutually exclusive boxes or a universal quality ranking. [S14]

Units and comparison matter

Separate the unit of observation from the unit of analysis. Thousands of events from ten people do not automatically create thousands of independent participants. Repeated observations, clustered organizations and temporal dependence affect inference. The sample is what you actually observe; the target population is what you want to say something about.

Probability sampling supports particular population inferences under its conditions. Purposive sampling selects informative cases for a stated inquiry. Convenience sampling uses what is available. None should be described as representative simply because the sample is large. Qualitative transferability also needs contextual reasoning, not an automatic claim that a theme holds everywhere.

Plan evaluation before collecting convenient evidence

Specify the comparison, measurement, analysis, exclusions and important failure modes. Use pilot work to test feasibility and refine procedures, clearly separating it from a confirmatory test of a prespecified claim. Reporting guidelines can help reveal missing decisions; they do not substitute for domain expertise or ethical approval. [S05] [S08]

Methodological fit outranks method prestige. An excellent interview cannot rescue a population-prevalence claim it was not designed to support.

Try it

Write why your chosen method can answer your question—and name the tempting conclusion it cannot support.
